# 800 to 1500 Lichess Rapid in 8-9 Months — Your Plan

Short version up front: **800 to 1500 is achievable in your timeframe at 30-45 min/day, but it's not automatic.** People who stall at low ratings almost always stall because they spend their time wrong — mostly on openings and watching content — not because they lack talent or hours. If you follow the allocation below, you'll almost certainly get there, and probably sooner than December.

The single most important sentence in this whole plan: **at your level, you lose games because you hang pieces and miss your opponent hanging pieces, not because of anything else.** Every hour of study should be judged against "does this make me blunder less or punish blunders more." That one lens will keep you from wasting months.

---

## The time allocation (this is the whole plan, really)

For your 30-45 min/day, use roughly this split:

| Activity                      | % of time | Daily minutes (of 40) |
|-------------------------------|-----------|------------------------|
| Tactics puzzles               | 40%       | ~15 min                |
| Playing rapid games (15+10)   | 30%       | ~12 min (one game)     |
| Reviewing your own games      | 20%       | ~8 min                 |
| Endgames + everything else    | 10%       | ~5 min                 |

Openings are deliberately not on that list. More on that below.

On days you only have 30 min: drop a game, do puzzles + review only. On days you have 45-60: add a second game or a longer review.

---

## What to actually do, activity by activity

### 1. Tactics puzzles (the #1 lever for you)

**At 800, roughly 90% of games are decided by one side hanging a piece or missing a tactic.** Puzzles train the pattern recognition that fixes both sides of that.

- Use **Lichess Puzzles** (free, unlimited, rated). Set the puzzle difficulty to "Normal" — it'll auto-adjust to your level.
- Do **15-25 puzzles per day**, every day. Consistency matters more than volume.
- **Don't rush.** For each puzzle: look at the whole board, identify all checks, captures, and threats for both sides, then calculate. Only move when you're sure. Guessing trains bad habits.
- If you fail a puzzle, **look at the solution, understand the pattern, then redo it mentally** before moving on.
- Optionally supplement with **Chess.com puzzles** or **Chessable's "1001 Chess Exercises for Beginners"** (Reinfeld-style) — but Lichess alone is fine.

Expect your puzzle rating to climb faster than your game rating. That's normal. The gap closes as you learn to actually apply patterns under the clock.

### 2. Playing games

Play **rapid only — specifically 15+10 or 10+5.** Do not play blitz or bullet regularly. Fast time controls at your level just reinforce pattern-matching on bad habits; you need time to actually calculate.

- **One serious rapid game per day** is the baseline.
- Before every move, run a quick checklist:
  1. What is my opponent's last move threatening?
  2. Are any of my pieces hanging (undefended or attacked)?
  3. Are any of their pieces hanging?
  4. What are all my checks, captures, and attacks?
  5. Then pick a move.

This sounds slow and obvious. At 800 rating, players skip steps 1-3 constantly — that's why they're 800. Doing this religiously is worth ~200 Elo on its own.

- **Don't tilt-queue.** If you lose 2 in a row and feel frustrated, stop. Come back tomorrow.
- **Don't play "just one more" at midnight.** Tired chess is negative ratings.

### 3. Reviewing your own games (the hidden superpower)

Most improvers skip this. It's probably the single highest-leverage thing you can do after puzzles.

After each rapid game (especially losses):

1. **First, review it yourself, without an engine.** Where do you think you went wrong? What was your opponent's plan?
2. **Then run Lichess's "Request Computer Analysis"** (free). Look specifically at:
   - Your **blunders (??)** and **mistakes (?)** — these are where your rating comes from.
   - Moves where the engine suggests something you didn't consider — ask "why is that better?"
3. **Ignore engine lines that go 8 moves deep with weird sacrifices.** You don't need to understand those. Focus on one-move and two-move mistakes.
4. Keep a simple notes file: "I keep hanging my knight on f6" or "I don't see back-rank threats." Patterns in your mistakes tell you what to work on.

5 minutes of honest review beats 30 minutes of opening videos. Every time.

### 4. Endgames

Endgames feel boring but they're absurdly high-ROI because (a) they come up every game, (b) almost nobody under 1500 studies them, and (c) the patterns are finite and memorizable.

At your level, you need to know cold:

- **King + Queen vs King** checkmate
- **King + Rook vs King** checkmate
- **King + Pawn vs King** — the concept of **opposition** and when pawns queen
- **Lucena** and **Philidor** rook endgames (later, around 1200+)
- Basic pawn endgame ideas: passed pawns, pawn majorities, the square of the pawn

Resource: **Lichess's "Practice" tab** has free interactive endgame drills. Do 5-10 minutes a few times a week. That's plenty.

### 5. Openings (do almost nothing here — this is counterintuitive but correct)

**This is the biggest trap for improving players.** You will be tempted to spend hours on openings. Don't. At 800-1500, games are decided by tactical mistakes in the middlegame, not opening prep. Your opponents won't know theory either.

What you actually need:

- **Pick one opening with White.** Recommend **the London System** (solid, few traps, same setup every game) or **the Italian Game** (classical, teaches good principles). Learn the first 6-8 moves and the basic ideas. 30 minutes total.
- **Pick one response to 1.e4 as Black.** Recommend **the Caro-Kann** (solid, forgiving) or **1...e5 into the Italian/Spanish** (most principled, but more theory).
- **Pick one response to 1.d4 as Black.** **The King's Indian Defense setup** (g6, Bg7, d6, Nf6, 0-0) works against most things at your level and has clear ideas.

Use Lichess's free **Opening Explorer** to see common moves in your positions. Don't buy a Chessable course yet. Don't memorize 20-move mainlines. Learn **principles**: control the center, develop all your pieces before attacking, castle early, connect your rooks, don't move the same piece twice in the opening without reason.

Revisit openings only when the same position keeps confusing you in game review. Let your actual games tell you what to study.

### 6. Content (streamers, YouTube, books) — supplemental only

This is entertainment that occasionally teaches. Don't let it replace the work above.

Good stuff, in rough priority:

- **GothamChess's "Guess the Elo" and "Beginner to Chess Master" series** — funny, genuinely instructive at your level.
- **Daniel Naroditsky's speedrun videos** — he narrates his thought process while playing up from beginner. Best free chess instruction on the internet, full stop.
- **John Bartholomew's "Chess Fundamentals" series** (YouTube, free) — short, clean lessons on undefended pieces, coordination, etc. Watch all of these. Seriously.
- **Books (if you like reading):**
  - *Logical Chess: Move by Move* by Chernev — perfect for your level.
  - *The Amateur's Mind* by Silman — slightly more advanced, aim for it around 1200.
  - Skip *My System* and other classics until 1600+.

Treat this as bonus — a video during lunch, not in place of your 40 minutes.

## The month-by-month arc

You don't need to overthink phases, but here's a reasonable progression. Adjust based on where you're stuck.

**Months 1-2 (April-May): Stop hanging pieces. Target ~1000.**
- Puzzles daily. Blunder-check every move.
- Play 15+10 exclusively.
- Review every loss. Log your recurring mistakes.
- Pick your openings (one hour total, never again this month).
- Learn K+Q vs K and K+R vs K mates.

**Months 3-4 (June-July): See tactics before they happen. Target ~1200.**
- Puzzles still the core. Start doing some "calculate 3 moves deep" training — Lichess has a puzzle streak mode.
- Start noticing opponent's threats before your own plans.
- Add basic pawn endgames (opposition, passed pawns).
- Watch one Naroditsky speedrun game per week and try to predict his moves.

**Months 5-6 (August-September): Real positional ideas. Target ~1350.**
- Keep puzzle volume up — this never stops mattering.
- Start thinking about **piece activity**, **weak squares**, **pawn structure** (not memorized, just noticing). Silman's *The Amateur's Mind* fits here.
- Learn Lucena and Philidor rook endgames.
- Review 2-3 of your wins too, not just losses — check whether you won because you played well or because they blundered.

**Months 7-9 (October-December): Polish. Target 1500.**
- By now, your rating gains slow — this is normal. You're no longer just stopping blunders; you're outplaying people.
- Expand opening knowledge a bit — know why the moves are played, not just what they are.
- Play a few longer time controls (30+0 or 30+20) occasionally to practice deeper calculation.
- Consider one lesson with a coach (~$30-50 on Chess.com or Lichess) to get a personalized diagnosis of weak spots. Totally optional but very high-ROI at this stage.

## Habits that will silently sink you (avoid)

- **Playing bullet/blitz to "warm up."** It doesn't warm you up, it trains you to move fast and not check for hanging pieces. If you want to play quick games for fun, do it — but don't count it as practice.
- **Watching 2 hours of chess YouTube and calling it studying.** It's not. It's entertainment.
- **Memorizing opening traps.** They work once, then you're in a worse middlegame.
- **Chasing the latest shiny opening repertoire.** Pick one, stick with it for months.
- **Playing when tilted.** Your rating graph will thank you.
- **Resigning too early.** At your level, opponents blunder constantly. Play on from worse positions — it's free rating.
- **Not using your clock.** If you have 15 minutes, use them. Taking 30 seconds on a critical move is often the difference between a win and a loss.

## How to measure progress

- **Primary metric: Lichess rapid rating.** Expect it to be jagged — plus or minus 100 points week to week is normal. Look at the 30-day trend.
- **Secondary: your puzzle rating.** It should climb faster.
- **Tertiary (most important qualitatively): are you blundering less?** Count the "??" marks in your game reviews. Watch that number trend down over months. That's real improvement, even if rating lags.

Set a weekly check-in with yourself (Sunday evening, 5 minutes): Did I hit my daily practice most days? What mistake keeps showing up in reviews? Adjust next week's focus accordingly.

---

## TL;DR, one more time

1. **15 min of puzzles daily.** Non-negotiable.
2. **One 15+10 rapid game daily, with a blunder-check before every move.**
3. **Review every loss briefly** — find the one move that lost it.
4. **Ignore openings beyond basics. Ignore blitz. Ignore YouTube rabbit holes.**
5. **A little endgame study each week.**
6. **Sleep. Don't play tilted.**

Do this for 8 months and 1500 is likely. Do it while watching streamers instead of doing the work, and you'll be 950 in December wondering why. The plan is simple; the discipline is the hard part.

Good luck — you picked a good hobby. See you at 1500.
