Yeah, this is a real and very common pattern, and the good news is there's nothing "wrong with you" — you're running a learning loop that almost everyone falls into because it feels productive. Let me tell you what's actually happening, then give you a concrete way out.

## The diagnosis: you're consuming, not practicing

What you've been doing in both Spanish and piano is the same thing:

1. **Passive input disguised as learning.** Watching YouTube videos about Spanish, watching videos about piano technique — this activates the "I'm learning!" feeling in your brain because you're encountering new information and understanding it in the moment. But understanding something when it's explained to you is wildly different from being able to produce it yourself under pressure. Recognition is not recall. Comprehension is not production.

2. **No feedback loop.** Duolingo gives you a tiny bit of feedback, but it's mostly pattern-matching multiple choice in a controlled environment. YouTube gives you zero feedback — the video doesn't know if you got it. Real skill acquisition requires a tight cycle of: attempt something slightly too hard → get corrected → try again. Without that, you're just rehearsing the feeling of learning.

3. **No measurable target.** "Learn Spanish" and "learn piano" are goals the size of a continent. You can't tell if you're making progress because you never defined what progress looks like. So you use proxies — streaks, hours watched, vocabulary app counts — and those proxies lie to you. When Mexico tested the real skill, the proxies collapsed.

4. **Ambition without a staircase.** Picking a Chopin piece because it sounds cool is the piano equivalent of trying to have a conversation in Mexico after three months of Duolingo. The gap between your current ability and the target is so huge that you can't actually work on the target — you can only fake-work on it (memorize a few bars, fumble through, feel bad). You need a skill one notch above where you are right now, not ten notches above.

5. **No protection against the dip.** Every skill has a phase where the novelty wears off, the plateau hits, and it stops feeling like magic. You've been quitting exactly at the moment real learning was about to start — because the "excited consuming videos" phase was the easy part, and the "grinding through fundamentals you're bad at" phase is where actual progress is made.

So the pattern is: **Consume → Feel like you're learning → Hit reality → Realize consumption didn't transfer → Quit.**

## The prescription

Here's a system that fixes all five failure modes. It's not glamorous, but it works for basically any skill.

### 1. Pick a concrete, testable outcome — not a topic

Instead of "learn Spanish," pick: *"In 90 days, I can have a 10-minute conversation with a tutor about my job, my weekend, and ordering food, without switching to English."* That's testable. You either can or you can't.

Instead of "learn piano," pick: *"In 90 days, I can play a Grade 1 piece cleanly at tempo, sight-read a simple 4-bar melody, and play all 12 major scales hands separately."* Also testable.

The target should be (a) specific, (b) slightly uncomfortable but achievable in the time window, and (c) something you can actually verify with a real-world test, not an app score.

### 2. Flip your input-to-output ratio

Aim for roughly **80% active production, 20% input**. That's the opposite of what you've been doing.

- Spanish: talking, writing, listening-and-responding. Not watching videos about grammar.
- Piano: playing, drilling, sight-reading. Not watching videos about technique.

Watching videos is fine as a small seasoning on top of practice. It cannot *be* the practice. The rule of thumb: if at the end of the session you couldn't show someone a thing you can now do that you couldn't do before, the session was probably consumption.

### 3. Get a feedback source — ideally a human

This is the single biggest lever. Options in order of effectiveness:

- **A tutor or teacher** (even cheap ones on iTalki for Spanish, or a local piano teacher for one 30-min lesson every 2 weeks). A tutor will catch errors you don't know you're making. Three months with a $15/hour iTalki tutor twice a week would have made your Mexico trip a completely different experience.
- **A structured curriculum that tests you.** For Spanish: something like Language Transfer, Pimsleur, or Dreaming Spanish paired with actual speaking practice. For piano: a method book like Alfred's or Faber, or a teacher-designed program. These force you up a staircase instead of letting you pick random shiny pieces.
- **Recording yourself and reviewing.** Record yourself speaking Spanish or playing piano. It's painful. It's also incredibly diagnostic — you hear the gap immediately.

Avoid: pure self-directed YouTube-hopping. It has no feedback loop.

### 4. Build a staircase of "just barely too hard" targets

The research term for this is the "zone of proximal development" — the skill level where a task is hard enough that you struggle but easy enough that you can do it with effort. That's where learning happens.

- Piano: don't pick Chopin. Pick a piece that's 80% achievable in a week of daily practice. Finish it. Pick another slightly harder one. Keep a list of completed pieces. In 6 months you'll have built actual ability, and *then* Chopin starts being plausible.
- Spanish: don't try to watch a telenovela at full speed. Find graded readers and comprehensible input at your level (Dreaming Spanish is built around exactly this idea — videos calibrated to A1, A2, B1, etc.). Move up when the current level feels comfortable.

The test for whether the target is right: you should fail about 15-30% of the time. If you never fail, it's too easy. If you fail more than half the time, it's too hard and you'll quit.

### 5. Practice daily, briefly, with a tight loop

Twenty focused minutes a day beats two hours on Saturday, every time. Skill acquisition is about consolidation between sessions — your brain does a huge amount of the work while you sleep, but only on things you actually attempted. Miss days and you lose that consolidation.

Structure for a practice session (either skill):
- 2 min: warmup / review the thing you worked on yesterday
- 10-15 min: work on the one specific thing that's currently just past your ability
- 3-5 min: attempt to use it in something real (free speaking, playing the piece end-to-end, etc.)

One thing per session. Not "work on Spanish." *"Today I'm drilling the preterite vs imperfect distinction with 30 example sentences out loud, then I'm telling my tutor about my weekend using both tenses."*

### 6. Track leading indicators, not vanity metrics

Bad metrics: Duolingo streak, hours of video watched, apps opened.

Good metrics:
- "Can I do X today that I couldn't do last week?"
- A weekly 5-minute self-test (record yourself answering a prompt, or play a piece cold).
- Tutor feedback / lesson progress.

Review weekly. If nothing measurably improved in a week, your practice structure is wrong, not your effort.

### 7. Pre-commit to pushing through the dip

You already know you quit around the few-weeks mark. Expect it. Literally write down: *"Around week 3-6 I will feel like I'm not making progress and want to quit. That feeling is the signal that real learning is starting, not the signal to stop."*

A useful rule: **decide in advance on a minimum commitment.** "I will do 20 minutes a day for 90 days, then re-evaluate." Not forever — 90 days. At 90 days, you'll either have visible progress (keep going) or you'll have real data about what's not working (adjust, don't quit the whole skill).

## The one-line version

**You've been confusing the feeling of learning (input, novelty, understanding-in-the-moment) with actual learning (producing, failing, getting corrected, trying again at slightly-above-your-level).** Fix the feedback loop, pick a testable target, practice daily in short focused sessions with something just past your edge, and pre-commit to the dip. Everything else is detail.

Pick one skill. Don't try to fix both at once. Start this week.
