# Myths and misconceptions

Common misconceptions about learning that the Math Academy book explicitly debunks. If a user cites one of these as a reason to change the plan, this reference explains what the research actually says.

## "I'm a visual / auditory / kinesthetic learner"

**What people mean.** They think they have a personal "learning style" and that matching instruction to that style improves learning.

**What the research shows.** Extensively tested and extensively *un*supported. Learning styles are one of the most persistent and well-studied *neuromyths* in education. Pashler, McDaniel, Rohrer & Bjork (2008) reviewed the literature and found essentially no evidence that matching instructional style to a student's self-reported preference improves outcomes. Follow-ups over the next decade have agreed.

**What's actually going on.** People do have *preferences* for how they consume information (and those preferences are real and worth respecting for motivation/engagement). But their actual learning outcomes depend on principles that are *domain-dependent, not person-dependent*: dual coding works for everyone, retrieval practice works for everyone, spacing works for everyone.

**What to do in a plan.** Don't design around a supposed style. Design around the structure of the subject itself (which modalities genuinely help — diagrams for geometry, audio for language, motor for instruments) and the universal strategies.

## "Some people are 'left-brained' (logical) and some are 'right-brained' (creative)"

Neuromyth. Brain imaging does show lateralization for some specific functions, but the "personality type" application is pop-psychology. The whole brain is involved in both analytical and creative work.

## "You have to learn the fundamentals completely before moving on"

**Half-truth.** Mastery learning requires clearing a proficiency bar on prerequisites before attempting dependent topics. This is correct.

**But the mistake** is to interpret this as "complete *all* foundational knowledge before starting the exciting course." In practice, that kills motivation and most learners quit during foundation-patching.

**What works better.** Start on the target topic. Work on the frontier topics that don't depend on the foundation gaps. When a specific gap blocks progress on a specific topic, patch *that* gap. Momentum is itself a resource worth preserving.

## "Productive struggle is good, so I should just sit with hard problems"

**Half-truth.** Desirable difficulties do improve learning, and learners should be working at the edge of their ability.

**But the mistake** is to confuse desirable difficulty with *insurmountable* difficulty. Bjork & Bjork (2023): *"If the learner does not have the background knowledge or skills to respond to them successfully, they become undesirable difficulties."*

**What works.** If you're stuck on a problem for 15 minutes with no progress, the task isn't at your frontier — a prerequisite is missing. Stop, back up, identify the missing component, drill it briefly, and return. Repeated struggle without resolution trains learned helplessness, not fluency.

## "Discovery learning is best — let them figure it out"

**What research shows.** Mayer (2004): "Should there be a three-strikes rule against pure discovery learning?" Extensive evidence that pure discovery learning / minimally-guided instruction produces significantly worse outcomes than *active learning with direct instruction*. The classic meta-analyses (Kirschner, Sweller & Clark; Mayer; Hattie) consistently rank direct instruction above discovery.

**The confusion.** Some studies have compared "active + unguided" to "passive + direct" and found active+unguided winning. This gets misread as "unguided is better." In reality, when you hold active constant, direct instruction beats unguided. The hierarchy is: *Active+Direct > Active+Unguided > Passive+Direct*.

**What to do.** Provide direct instruction (worked examples, clear explanations) AND active practice (lots of production with feedback). Don't pretend that letting the learner flail is pedagogically superior.

## "Teaching general problem-solving skills is the path to being a good problem-solver"

**What research shows.** Sweller, Clark & Kirschner (2010): "Teaching general problem-solving skills is not a substitute for, or a viable addition to, teaching mathematics." There is a mountain of evidence that you improve problem-solving in a domain by acquiring more foundational skills *in that domain*, not by doing abstract "thinking skills" exercises.

**Why.** Expert problem-solvers don't use general heuristics — they match the current problem against thousands of similar problems they've previously solved. A chess grandmaster has 50,000+ chess-specific patterns. A fluent Spanish speaker has thousands of sentence patterns. General-purpose thinking is a very weak substitute.

**Implication for plans.** Don't carve out time for "problem-solving drills in general." Carve out time for lots of domain-specific problems.

## "If I can follow along, I understand it"

**What research shows.** Following along uses *recognition*, which is far easier than *recall*. You can follow a textbook proof perfectly and then be unable to reconstruct it five minutes later. Francis Bacon noticed this in 1620.

**Test yourself.** If you haven't closed the book and tried to produce the material from memory, you don't know whether you know it. Usually you don't.

## "Cramming worked fine in school"

**What research shows.** Cramming produces short-term fluency that decays fast. In the Gordon (1925) study, the massed-practice group actually *beat* the spaced group on the immediate test, then lost to them badly on the 4-week delayed test. 254 studies since confirm: cramming is decent for exams tomorrow, terrible for retention past a few days.

**Why people believe it "worked."** The exam was tomorrow, and it did work for the exam. Nobody measured what they retained a year later. Usually almost nothing.

## "I need quiet / music / a specific environment to study"

**What research shows.** *Varying* the conditions of learning, rather than keeping them constant and predictable, is one of Bjork's desirable difficulties. Studying in multiple environments, at multiple times of day, with varying distractions actually improves retention and transfer.

**The intuition trap.** Studying in a constant environment makes the practice feel easier (consistency reduces cognitive load during practice), but the resulting memory is cue-dependent on that environment — when the cues change (e.g., test day), recall suffers.

## "Timed tests are always anxiety-inducing and should be avoided"

**What research shows.** There's very little evidence that timed tests themselves cause anxiety. What causes anxiety is a *mismatch* between the student's skill level and the test's demands — being timed on something you can't yet do accurately.

**What actually helps with math anxiety.** (a) Building proficiency through more practice; (b) promoting a growth mindset via language ("my brain gets stronger when I work it"). Avoiding tests altogether prevents the proficiency from developing.

**In a plan.** Don't use timing to introduce new skills. Once a skill is reliable untimed, add timing to build fluency. Gunderson et al. (2018) found that skill-building + growth mindset reduces math anxiety; avoidance does not.

## "I'm just not a math person / language person / music person"

The honest version of this statement is *"I have not yet put in the deliberate practice needed to develop this skill."* Ericsson's research consistently finds that individual differences in performance are dominated by the amount of deliberate practice, even among elite performers. Innate differences exist, but they are a small factor compared to consistent, effortful, feedback-driven practice over years.

This is not a moral judgment. It's a statement that the plan can work.

## "Working memory / attention span / intelligence is fixed"

**What research shows.** Working memory capacity *per se* is relatively stable, but the effective capacity *within a domain* grows enormously with expertise (Chase & Ericsson, 1982). A chess grandmaster's working memory hasn't gotten bigger in an absolute sense — it's that their long-term memory is so densely organized around chess patterns that long-term memory effectively extends their working memory. The same applies to any deeply-learned domain.

**What this means.** Being "overwhelmed" by a topic today is evidence that you haven't built the chunked representations yet, not evidence of a fixed ceiling.

## "I'll learn better once I find the right textbook / course / video"

Resource-shopping is usually a form of productive-seeming procrastination. The differences between good resources in a domain are small compared to the difference between *actually doing the work* and *not*. Pick one reasonable primary resource and commit. If the resource genuinely fails you (you can't understand it, it lacks exercises, feedback isn't available), switch once. Don't keep switching.

## "Reviewing right after learning is the best way to remember it"

**What research shows.** Immediate review produces very small additional memory gains because memory is already maxed. The spacing effect shows that review *after some forgetting has begun* is where the gains come from — reconstruction requires effort, and effort is what consolidates memory further.

**What to do.** Don't re-read a chapter right after reading it. Close the book, do something else, and come back the next day with retrieval practice.

## "Some people just learn faster than others, so I shouldn't compare myself"

**What's true and what isn't.** Learning speeds do differ between individuals — primarily as a function of working memory capacity and prior domain knowledge. Someone starting fresh on linear algebra after years of math practice will absorb it faster than someone starting fresh after years of no math, and this is a real effect.

**But the common misuse is to skip to a conclusion that doesn't follow:** "so I shouldn't bother pushing myself" or "so I can't really learn this" or "so the bar should be lower for me." None of those follow. What does follow from the speed-difference finding:

- Faster learners and slower learners both need to clear the *same bar* of proficiency to actually use a skill. The bar doesn't shift based on how long it took to reach. 
- The right adjustment for slower learning speed is to **increase practice volume** or **extend the timeline** — not to lower the standard of mastery.
- For almost everyone, the *ceiling* on how much they could eventually learn is much higher than where they actually end up. The gap is almost always caused by missing foundations, ineffective practice methods, insufficient total practice volume, or motivation collapse — not innate capacity.

**What to do.** Don't let a self-diagnosis of "I'm a slow learner" justify reducing the mastery bar. Instead, if practice is harder than expected, add scaffolding, split topics more finely, and extend the schedule. The direction that bends is time, not standard.

## "Learning should feel easy / effortless when you're doing it right"

**What research shows.** Effortful practice is a *defining feature* of deliberate practice, not a sign that something is wrong. Ericsson's core finding: the most effective practice is effortful, uncomfortable, and not inherently enjoyable. People sustain it because it improves performance, not because it feels good.

This is a place where intuition and research point opposite directions. The intuitive belief — "if learning feels hard, I'm doing it wrong / I'm just not suited for this / there must be a better method" — leads directly to abandoning effective practice techniques in favor of more comfortable but less productive ones. The technique that feels the worst during practice (interleaving, retrieval without notes, spaced review when you've half-forgotten) is often the one that produces the best long-term results.

**A common companion myth:** *"Competition is bad — it stresses learners out and we should avoid it."* There's little empirical support for this, and in practice, mild competitive structures (leagues, rank-order feedback, comparison to personal bests) tend to sustain motivation through hard phases rather than harm learners. What's actually harmful is *mismatched difficulty* — competition against vastly stronger opponents with no chance of succeeding — not competition itself. Matched competition is largely protective.

**What to do.** When the learner reports that practice feels hard, check whether the discomfort is desirable-difficulty (effortful but achievable) or genuine mismatch (insurmountable, or a missing prerequisite). The first is the plan working as intended. The second is a signal to add scaffolding. Neither is a signal that the learner is fundamentally unsuited.

## "Acceleration hurts students — they go too fast and don't learn deeply"

**What research shows.** This belief is widespread in education but lacks empirical support. Acceleration — letting students progress at their natural pace rather than being held to the grade-level schedule — doesn't harm psychological outcomes, doesn't produce shallower learning, and doesn't leave accelerated learners "burned out" or "running out of material."

Specifically:

- Accelerated students learn *the same material at the same depth*, just faster, because the pacing assumption in traditional schooling was an artifact of group instruction logistics, not a requirement of learning.
- Accelerated students don't run out of things to learn. In mathematics, for example, there is far more university-level math above calculus than there is K–12 math below it — the apparent "ceiling" is illusory.
- Advanced placement in any domain tends to open more doors (access to harder courses, better mentors, career pathways) and correlates with stronger lifetime achievement, not burnout.

**The common misuse.** Adult learners (and parents of learners) sometimes hold themselves back on the belief that "I should fully master every layer before moving on, otherwise I'm going to have shaky foundations." The half-truth here is real — you do need to master prerequisites before advancing — but the misuse is to confuse *mastery of prerequisites* with *taking the expected calendar time*. If the learner has mastered the prerequisites, advancing sooner than the calendar schedule is not a risk; it's the right move.

**What to do.** Don't assume a faster-than-expected pace is a red flag. If milestone checks are passing and retention is holding, the learner is not "going too fast" — they're learning at their natural pace, which is what talent-development environments are designed to allow.
