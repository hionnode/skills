# 800 → 1500 Lichess Rapid in 8–9 Months: A Concrete Plan

Good news first: 800 → 1500 on Lichess rapid in 8–9 months with 30–45 min/day is very achievable. Most players who actually do the work get there faster. The people who get stuck at 800–1200 usually aren't doing "the wrong things" — they're doing a scattered mix of everything (random openings, watching streamers, some puzzles) without a feedback loop. You need a feedback loop.

Below is the actual plan: what to do each day, why, and how it changes as you improve.

---

## The core principle: how you actually gain rating below ~1800

At your level, games are decided almost entirely by **blunders** — pieces hanging, missed one-move tactics, walking into forks. Openings and endgames matter *far* less than you think. The player who blunders less wins. Period.

So the engine of improvement is:

1. **Play slow-ish games** (so you have time to not blunder).
2. **Analyze every loss** to find the blunder and understand it.
3. **Do tactics puzzles daily** to train pattern recognition so you blunder less and spot opponents' blunders more.
4. **Learn a tiny, safe opening repertoire** just so you don't get crushed in 10 moves.
5. **Learn basic endgames** so you can convert won positions.

That's it. That's the whole game up to 1500. Everything else — streamers, opening theory, fancy books — is either optional entertainment or a distraction at your level.

---

## The daily/weekly split (30–45 min)

Here's a realistic split that fits your time budget. Don't overthink it — roughly hit these ratios each week:

**Every day (~20–30 min):**
- **10–15 min of tactics puzzles** on Lichess (Puzzles → start with the default rating-matched ones). This is non-negotiable. It's the single highest-ROI activity you can do.
- **One 15-minute rapid game** (10+0 or 15+10 on Lichess). Play it seriously, no moving instantly, no music blasting.

**After the game (5–10 min):**
- **Analyze it.** Run Lichess's "Request Computer Analysis" / "Learn from your mistakes" feature. Find the moment you blundered (or your opponent did and you missed it). Understand *why*. Move on.

**Twice a week (replaces one game day):**
- **20–25 min structured study** — rotate between opening basics, endgame basics, and a beginner-friendly book/course (see below).

**Once a week (optional but strongly recommended):**
- **One longer 30-minute rapid game (30+0 or 30+20)** played on a fresh brain. This is where most of your learning happens because you actually have time to calculate.

That's it. If you miss a day, don't double up — just resume.

## What to actually do in each bucket

### 1. Tactics (your #1 tool)

**Platform:** Lichess Puzzles. Free, unlimited, rating-matched. Don't pay for Chess.com or ChessTempo; Lichess's puzzles are excellent.

**How to do them properly** — this matters:
- **Don't guess.** If you don't see the answer, spend up to 60–90 seconds actually calculating before you move.
- **Say the idea out loud** before you move: "If I play Nxf7, king is forced to take, then Qh5+ forks king and knight." If you can't articulate *why* the move works, don't play it.
- **Getting them wrong is fine.** Getting them wrong *fast* without thinking is how people spend years at 800. Slow down.

**Volume:** 10–20 puzzles/day is plenty. Quality > quantity.

**When you've plateaued on rating-matched puzzles,** try Lichess's "Puzzle Storm" once a week for speed pattern recognition, and "Puzzle Themes → Mate in 2" / "Fork" / "Pin" to drill specific patterns.

### 2. Playing games

**Time control:** **10+0 or 15+10 rapid.** Not bullet. Not blitz. Bullet and blitz will actively make you worse at 800 because you don't have time to think — you're just training bad reflexes.

**Rule of thumb:** if you want a rating of X, play a time control slow enough that you can actually calculate two moves deep. For 800→1500, that's 10+0 minimum, 15+10 ideal.

**Games per day:** 1–2. More is fine on weekends but diminishing returns. Playing 10 games a day without analyzing any of them is how people stay at 800 forever.

**The blunder-check habit:** Before every move, ask yourself two questions:
1. "What is my opponent threatening?" (What did their last move attack or set up?)
2. "If I make this move, what can they capture? Is anything of mine hanging?"

Do this *every single move*. Not just when it feels dangerous. This one habit alone is worth ~300 rating points.

### 3. Analyzing your games (underrated, huge)

After each game:
1. Click **"Request Computer Analysis"** on Lichess.
2. Use **"Learn from your mistakes"** — it walks you through your blunders and asks you to find the better move.
3. For each blunder, ask: *was it tactical (I missed a piece hanging / a fork) or strategic (bad plan)?* At your level, ~90% will be tactical. That's fine — it tells you puzzles are the right focus.
4. Don't spend more than 10 minutes on this. You're not doing deep GM prep; you're just catching the pattern.

**Keep a "blunder log" (optional but powerful):** a Notes app file with one line per game: *"Hung knight to a fork on move 18 — didn't see Nxe5 attacking queen and knight."* After a month, patterns emerge. You'll see you keep missing the same type of tactic — now you know what to drill.

### 4. Openings (do the minimum)

**Do NOT buy a Chessable course yet. Do NOT study opening theory deeply. Do NOT memorize 15 moves of the Najdorf.**

At 800–1500, you need:
- **As White:** pick *one* opening. Recommendation: **the London System** (1.d4 2.Nf3 3.Bf4 4.e3 5.Bd3 6.c3 7.Nbd2) or the **Italian Game** (1.e4 e5 2.Nf3 Nc6 3.Bc4). Both are forgiving, teach good piece placement, and have simple plans.
- **As Black vs 1.e4:** either the **Caro-Kann** (1…c6) or just **1…e5** (play symmetric). Caro-Kann is more forgiving.
- **As Black vs 1.d4:** the **Slav** (1…d5 2.c4 c6) or the **King's Indian setup** (1…Nf6, 2…g6, 3…Bg7, 4…d6). Pick one.

Learn the **first 6–8 moves and the basic plan** for each. That's it. "lichess.org/opening" and the free **Chessable "Short & Sweet"** courses are perfect for this — they take ~1 hour total.

Spend maybe 2–3 hours *total* on openings across the whole 8 months. Seriously. Every hour on openings below 1500 is an hour you could've spent on tactics, which would've gained you more rating.

### 5. Endgames (also do the minimum, but do it)

You need to know, cold:
- **King + Queen vs King** mate
- **King + Rook vs King** mate
- **King + Pawn vs King** — the concept of the **opposition** and the **square of the pawn**
- **Lucena and Philidor positions** (rook endings) — learn these around rating 1200+

Resource: **"Silman's Complete Endgame Course"** (first 2–3 chapters) OR just watch **John Bartholomew's "Chess Fundamentals" YouTube series** (free, excellent).

Budget: ~3–4 hours total across the 8 months. You're not becoming an endgame expert; you're just making sure you can convert a won position and not stalemate yourself.

### 6. Study resources (pick ONE, don't collect them)

In rough order of ROI for your level:

1. **John Bartholomew's "Climbing the Rating Ladder" YouTube series** — free, he plays against real players at specific rating levels and narrates his thinking. Watching him play an 800-rated opponent shows you exactly what you're doing wrong. Start here.
2. **John Bartholomew's "Chess Fundamentals" series** — free, covers undefended pieces, coordinates, pattern recognition. This is probably the single best free resource for 800→1400.
3. **"Logical Chess: Move by Move" by Irving Chernev** — classic beginner book. Every move in 33 games is explained. Slow, but builds deep understanding. Read 1 game per week.
4. **Aman Hambleton's "Building Habits" series (Chessbrah, YouTube)** — he plays with strict rules ("never hang a piece, always develop") and climbs from 800 to 2000. Shows you what a *principled* game looks like at every level.
5. **ChessDojo's free training program** — they have a rated training plan per rating band. Very structured if you want more guidance.

**Do NOT** try to read Silman's "How to Reassess Your Chess" or Dvoretsky or any Soviet classics yet. They're for 1600+ and will confuse you.

### 7. Streamers: entertainment, not study

Hikaru, GothamChess, Eric Rosen, etc. — fun to watch, almost zero rating gain per hour. If you like it, watch, but count it as entertainment, not study time. **Levy Rozman's "How to Win at Chess" beginner videos are an exception** — genuinely instructive.

## The month-by-month trajectory (rough)

Rating gain isn't linear — it plateaus, then jumps. Don't panic during plateaus. Rough milestones:

- **Month 1–2 (April–May): 800 → ~1000.** You stop hanging pieces every game. Biggest single jump will come from the blunder-check habit alone.
- **Month 3–4 (June–July): 1000 → ~1200.** Tactics start clicking. You spot 1-move tactics reliably and 2-move combinations sometimes. First opening feels comfortable.
- **Month 5–6 (August–September): 1200 → ~1350.** Endgames start mattering. Positional ideas (controlling open files, weak squares) start to register.
- **Month 7–8 (October–November): 1350 → ~1500.** You play fewer blunders, convert more won positions. Games get longer because neither side collapses in the middlegame.
- **Month 9 (December): buffer / push to 1500 if you're not there yet.**

If you're ahead of pace, great — keep doing the same thing, don't get fancy. If you're behind, the single most common reason is: **not enough slow games, or not analyzing the games you play.** Not "the wrong opening" or "not enough theory."

## Things that will stall you — watch for these

1. **Playing blitz/bullet when you feel like you don't have 15 minutes.** Just skip the day. Five blitz games teach you nothing; one rapid game teaches you something.
2. **Not analyzing losses because they're painful.** The losses are where the rating points are. Ten minutes of analysis per loss > another game played.
3. **Opening rabbit holes.** The moment you catch yourself watching a 45-minute video on the Najdorf Sicilian, close it. You'll never see that position.
4. **Tilt.** If you lose 2 in a row, stop for the day. Playing tilted is worse than not playing.
5. **Rating obsession.** Don't check your rating after every game. Check weekly. The daily noise is huge and demoralizing.
6. **Chess.com vs Lichess confusion.** Pick one platform and stick to it. Chess.com ratings run ~200 points lower than Lichess for the same strength — they're not comparable. Since you're on Lichess, stay on Lichess. 1500 Lichess rapid ≈ 1300 Chess.com rapid, roughly.

## Your first week — concrete starting plan

Don't overthink Day 1. Do this:

- **Today:** Pick your openings (London as White, Caro-Kann vs 1.e4, Slav vs 1.d4). Spend 30 min on Lichess's free "Opening Explorer" clicking through the first 6 moves of each.
- **Tomorrow:** Play one 15+10 rapid game. Analyze it. Do 10 puzzles.
- **Day 3:** Watch Episode 1 of John Bartholomew's "Chess Fundamentals" (YouTube, free, ~20 min). Do 10 puzzles.
- **Day 4:** One rapid game + analysis. 10 puzzles.
- **Day 5:** Watch Bartholomew Episode 2. 10 puzzles.
- **Day 6:** One rapid game + analysis. 10 puzzles.
- **Day 7:** One longer 30+20 game on fresh brain. Analyze thoroughly.

After week 1, you'll have a feel for the rhythm. Adjust from there.

---

## The one-sentence version

**Play one 15+10 rapid game a day, analyze it, do 10–15 puzzles, and every move ask yourself "what's hanging?" — that alone gets you to 1500.**

Everything else is optimization around those four habits. Good luck — you'll get there well before December if you stay consistent.
