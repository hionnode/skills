# Chess: 800 → 1500 Lichess Rapid in 8–9 months

You've given me enough to build a real plan. 30–45 min/day, 800 rapid, basic tactics, 8–9 months. 1500 is a realistic target on that budget if you spend the time on the right things. Most people at 800 plateau because they spend their time on the wrong things — watching streamers, grinding openings, playing bullet. You do not need any of that.

Here's what's actually going on at your level: games at 800–1500 are decided almost entirely by **one-move blunders and missed one-to-three-move tactics**. Not by opening theory. Not by deep positional ideas. Not by endgame technique (though a tiny bit helps). If you stop hanging pieces and start seeing the tactics your opponent is hanging, you will be 1500. Every hour spent on anything else is, at best, the 4th most valuable hour you could have spent.

That insight drives the whole plan.

---

## Goal

By end of year (roughly 8 months from now): **1500 Lichess rapid**, stable — not a one-off peak but a rating floor you hold for 2+ weeks. Operationally: you stop hanging pieces in >95% of games, you spot basic tactics (pins/forks/skewers/discoveries/removing the defender/back rank) within 10 seconds when they're on the board, and you don't lose "won" positions to random tactics in the endgame.

## Starting point (what I'm assuming — correct me if wrong)

- 800 Lichess rapid. Fine for this plan.
- Know the rules and basic tactic names (pin, fork).
- Likely weaknesses at this level (take these as probabilistic, not certain): you play too fast and don't check for your opponent's threats before moving; you don't have a consistent opening you play every game; you don't know basic checkmate patterns cold; you don't have a checklist before moves; you mostly play, rarely review.
- No real endgame knowledge. That's fine for now.

## The big idea: where your time goes

Given your 30–45 min/day budget, here is roughly where every minute should go. This is very different from what most 800s do.

| Activity | % of time | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Tactics puzzles (Lichess or Chess.com, calibrated to your level) | ~40% | Single highest-ROI activity at your level. Pattern recognition is the bottleneck. |
| Playing *slow* rapid games (15+10 or 10+5), with a blunder-check routine | ~25% | You need to practice the actual skill, under time pressure, with feedback. |
| Reviewing your own games (especially losses) with the engine | ~20% | This is the feedback loop. Without it you repeat the same mistakes forever. |
| A tiny bit of endgame + mating-pattern study | ~10% | Checkmate patterns = tactics, basically. Plus a handful of king+pawn endings. |
| One opening system for White, one for each of e4/d4 as Black — *very shallow* | ~5% | Enough to not hemorrhage time in the opening. That's all you need at this level. |

Notice what's missing: streamers, opening books, puzzle rush/streak, bullet, blitz, and "studying grandmaster games." These are not bad hobbies but they are not the work. Skip them, at least while you're chasing 1500.

## The phased roadmap

Each phase is ~4–5 weeks with a clear milestone. If you hit a milestone early, move up. If not, don't bash on — check the weak component and do targeted work.

### Phase 1 (Weeks 1–4): Stop hanging pieces. Build the blunder-check habit.

**Goal:** stop losing games because of one-move blunders. Establish a pre-move checklist that becomes automatic.

- Pick **one opening system for White** (I recommend the **London System**: 1. d4, 2. Nf3, 3. Bf4, 4. e3, 5. Bd3, 6. c3, 7. Nbd2 — roughly the same setup every game). Memorize only the move order and the handful of standard responses to early tricks (…Qb6 hitting b2, …Nh5 hitting the bishop). Do not go deeper than move 8.
- Pick **one response to 1. e4** (I recommend the **Caro-Kann**: 1…c6 2…d5. Solid, low-theory, hard to beat you in the opening) and **one response to 1. d4** (play …d5 and head for a solid QGD-like setup, or just play the London colors-reversed).
- 5 minutes per day skimming Lichess's opening explorer for *your* games in *your* lines is plenty. Do not read opening books. Do not watch 2-hour YouTube opening videos.
- Install the blunder-check habit. Before every move, three questions, in order:
  1. What is my opponent threatening? (checks, captures, attacks — in that order)
  2. If I play my candidate move, does it leave any of my pieces undefended, attacked, or pinned? Is my king safe?
  3. Is there a better move — specifically a check, capture, or threat I'm missing?
- Milestone at end of phase 1: blunder rate (per Lichess game analysis, under "Blunders") drops below ~2 per game on average. Puzzle rating up by ~150 points.

### Phase 2 (Weeks 5–9): Tactical pattern recognition.

**Goal:** solve basic tactics cold. Recognize pin / fork / skewer / discovered attack / double attack / removing the defender / overloaded piece / back rank / smothered mate on sight.

- Grind Lichess puzzles at your calibrated rating, untimed, ~15 min/day.
- Add a **"puzzle themes"** cycle: rotate one theme per day (Monday: pins. Tuesday: forks. Wednesday: back rank. etc.) for the first 2 weeks — this is blocked practice to build the pattern. Then switch to mixed (interleaved) puzzles for the next 3 weeks — this is where it actually sticks.
- Add 10 min/day on **mating patterns**: Anastasia, Arabian, Boden's, smothered, back-rank, ladder. Use the Lichess "Checkmate" puzzle pack or a book like *Winning Chess Tactics* / *1001 Chess Exercises for Beginners* (Bain, van Wijgerden).
- Milestone: puzzle rating around 1600–1800 on Lichess (Lichess puzzle rating runs higher than game rating; that's fine). You can name the tactic you just spotted *before* you play the move.

### Phase 3 (Weeks 10–16): Playing stronger, thinking better.

**Goal:** convert the tactical vision into actual rating points. Introduce basic endgames and basic middlegame plans.

- Shift ratio slightly: more games, same tactics. 15 min tactics, 20 min games, 10 min review.
- Play only **15+10 rapid**. No blitz, no bullet. At 15+10 your blunder-check routine has time to fire. At blitz it doesn't.
- Play at most **2 rapid games/day**. You're not trying to pad volume. Quality > quantity.
- **Review every loss** (and at least every other win) with Lichess's free engine analysis. Find the moment the eval swung 1+ points. Name the tactic or concept you missed. Add it to a running note.
- Add **basic endgames**: king + queen vs. king, king + rook vs. king, the "square of the pawn" rule, opposition, Lucena, Philidor. Don't overdo it — 5–10 minutes 3× a week for one month covers this.
- Milestone at end of phase: ~1200 rapid.

### Phase 4 (Weeks 17–22): Middlegame plans and calculation depth.

**Goal:** not just "don't blunder" — actually have an idea. Start calculating 2–3 moves ahead reliably.

- Introduce **calculation exercises**: harder tactics puzzles (puzzle rating +200 above current), timed at 2–3 min/puzzle, force yourself to calculate the full line before moving. A good resource: *Chess Tactics from Scratch* (Weteschnik) or the "hardest" puzzles on Lichess.
- Start learning **basic middlegame ideas** tied to your openings: in the London, where do the pieces want to go? What is the minority attack? In the Caro-Kann, what are the typical pawn breaks (…c5, …e5)? One 30-min session per week reading/watching on *your* opening's middlegame, then finding an example in your own games.
- Milestone: ~1350 rapid.

### Phase 5 (Weeks 23–32): Polish and consolidate.

**Goal:** cross 1500 and stay there.

- Ratios stay roughly the same but the puzzles get harder and the review gets deeper — you're now looking not just at blunders but at positional misjudgments and missed plans.
- Play one **longer game** per week (30+0 or 30+20). This is where you practice deep thinking without the rapid-clock safety net.
- Optional: start reading a real positional book — Silman's *Reassess Your Chess*, or Aagaard's *Positional Play*. Not before now. At 1500, ideas about weak squares, outposts, and pawn structures start paying off. Before 1500, they don't — tactics dominate.
- Milestone: stable 1500 (rating > 1500 at the end of a two-week window, not just a spike).

## The daily session template

Pick one of two layouts depending on whether you have 30 or 45 minutes that day. Same structure either way.

### The 45-minute day

- **0–5 min: Warm-up retrieval.** Redo 3–5 puzzles you got wrong in the last few days, from memory. Open your "missed puzzles" list, look at the position, solve it cold, check. This is spaced review and it is where retention compounds — do not skip this when busy.
- **5–20 min: New tactics.** 10–15 fresh Lichess puzzles at your rating. Untimed. Before each move, verbalize (silently is fine, out loud is better) what you're calculating. When wrong: **do not immediately see the solution** — reset, look again for 60 seconds, only then reveal. Add any missed puzzle to the retrieval list for tomorrow.
- **20–40 min: One 15+10 rapid game.** Blunder-check routine on every move. No exceptions — if you catch yourself moving on autopilot, that's the moment the training happens.
- **40–45 min: Review the game.** Lichess engine analysis. Find the first move where eval swung by ≥1 point in either direction. In one sentence, write what you missed. Tag the tactic or concept. Done.

### The 30-minute day

- **0–5 min: Warm-up retrieval** (same as above).
- **5–20 min: Tactics.** 10 puzzles.
- **20–30 min: Either** one 5+5 game with strict blunder-check, **or** review yesterday's game properly.
- Alternate these days: game one day, deep review the next.

### The skip-a-day day

- If you truly can't, do 5 minutes of spaced-review puzzles on your phone. The habit matters more than the volume.

## Weekly rhythm

- **6 weekdays:** normal session.
- **1 day per week (pick one):** "deep review day." No puzzles, no new games. Pick 2–3 of your recent losses and go through them move by move with the engine. Write down the pattern of your mistakes — are they tactical, time-pressure, opening confusion, endgame technique? That pattern dictates what you overweight next week.
- **Every 4 weeks:** a "milestone check." Play 4 rapid games in one sitting at a slower time control (30+20). Look for: are the Phase X behaviors sticking? Is the blunder rate down? Are you seeing tactics? Adjust the plan.

## Resources (pick these, stop browsing)

One of each category. Do not stack alternatives.

- **Puzzles:** Lichess puzzles (free, huge database, calibrated). Nothing else needed. If you want a book: *1001 Chess Exercises for Beginners* by Bain / van Wijgerden (bite-size, structured by theme).
- **Play:** Lichess rapid. Avoid Chess.com bullet arenas — they're candy.
- **Review:** Lichess's built-in engine analysis. Free. Sufficient.
- **Opening ref (total, for the whole 8 months):** ChessBook or Lichess Studies for a London System repertoire and a Caro-Kann repertoire. 15 lines each, maximum. Do not exceed.
- **Endgame (one book total):** Silman's *Complete Endgame Course*, read only the sections flagged for your rating band. Do not read the whole book.
- **Mating patterns:** Lichess "Checkmate" puzzle theme, or the free site *chesstempo.com*.
- **Do not consume:** streamers (entertainment), GM game databases (over your head), Chess.com lessons (expensive, slow), grandmaster opening videos (irrelevant at your level).

If you want *one* YouTube channel as very occasional side reading: **Daniel Naroditsky's speedrun series**, specifically his sub-1500 rapid games where he verbalizes his thought process. Cap at 30 min/week. It is not a substitute for anything. It is a supplement to thinking.

## The feedback loop (this is the part most people skip)

Every game you lose contains the answer to why you're not 1500 yet. The review habit is non-negotiable.

- After every loss (and ideally every game): run engine analysis, find the first move where the eval dropped by ≥1.0, and write one line in a running notes file: **position → what you played → what you should have played → the tactic/concept you missed.**
- Once a week, skim those lines. Patterns will emerge quickly. "I keep missing knight forks" or "I lose all my endgames" or "I rush in time pressure and hang the queen." The patterns dictate next week's emphasis.
- Every missed puzzle goes into a list you re-attempt the next day, then 3 days later, then a week later. Lichess has this built in ("puzzle storm" is not this — look at your puzzle history and re-attempt misses).
- Every 4 weeks, sanity-check: is rapid rating trending up? If not, you are almost certainly either (a) not reviewing games, or (b) playing too fast (bullet/blitz pollution). Both are recoverable.

## Anti-patterns to watch for — by name

At 800, there are specific traps you will fall into. Calling them out so you recognize them in the wild.

- **"Just one more blitz game."** Blitz rewards pattern recognition you already have; it does not build pattern recognition you lack. At your level it's empty calories. Rapid only.
- **Opening rabbit holes.** You will be tempted to watch a 45-minute video on the Najdorf or the Grünfeld because it looks cool. Your next 1000 games will be decided by whether you see a fork, not by move 14 of the Najdorf. Five minutes of opening per day, cap.
- **Watching chess instead of playing chess.** Streamers are fun; they are not training. If you watch, watch *yourself review your own games* — that's training.
- **Puzzle Rush / Puzzle Streak.** Timed, adrenaline-driven puzzle-solving short-circuits the calculation habit you're trying to build. Use untimed puzzles until your rating is much higher.
- **Playing when tilted.** Losing 3 in a row, then playing 5 more to "get it back." All 5 will be bad games. Stop, walk away, come back tomorrow.
- **Resigning too early — or too late.** Both are feedback-loop failures. Play out losses to the bitter end for the early weeks, so you learn endgames and so your opponents actually have to convert. But: if a position is genuinely lost against a competent opponent, resign and review — don't burn 10 more minutes on a 0% position.
- **Skipping review because you won.** You miss tactics in your wins too. The wins teach you what worked, the losses teach you what didn't. Review both, lightly — losses in more depth.
- **Memorizing puzzle solutions.** If you see "pin on the d-file, done" and click the move without calculating, the puzzle taught you nothing. Always calculate the full line before moving, even when you think you see the answer.
- **Skipping the blunder-check when tired.** The entire plan hinges on the blunder-check becoming automatic. If you catch yourself skipping it, end the session — a bad game under autopilot installs bad habits.

## On the feel

For the first 2–4 weeks this plan will feel slow and annoying. You will play fewer games than before. Each game will take longer because you're actually checking for threats. The puzzles will feel harder than blitz because you're forced to calculate rather than pattern-match on vibes. **All of that is the point.**

Real improvement in chess is non-linear. You will plateau — often for 2–3 weeks — and then jump 100–150 points in a week. During the plateau, do not change the plan. The plateau is usually the pattern consolidating under the surface. If a plateau lasts more than 4 weeks, *then* diagnose: check your game review notes for a pattern you haven't addressed, and target that.

Also: do not check your rating after every game. Check it once a week, on your weekly review day. Rating variance at rapid is ±50 points on any given day. Watching it move around live is a distraction from the actual work.

## The one-paragraph summary

Every day: redo yesterday's missed puzzles (5 min), do ~15 min of fresh tactics at your level, play one 15+10 rapid game with a strict pre-move blunder-check, and review the game for the one moment the eval swung. Once a week: review 2–3 losses in depth and look for patterns. Pick the London + Caro-Kann + solid …d5 against 1. d4, memorize 5 moves deep, and ignore openings otherwise. Add mating patterns in month 2 and basic endgames in month 3. Stay off bullet, blitz, streamers, and opening theory. Keep it boring. 1500 by December.
