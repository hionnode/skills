# What's going wrong, and what to do instead

Short version: you're not actually learning in those first few weeks. You're *consuming*, which feels like learning because the material becomes familiar, and then you're shocked when you try to *produce* (speak Spanish in Mexico, play a piece on piano) and nothing is there. This is one of the most well-documented traps in the cognitive science of learning. It has a name — the **illusion of comprehension** — and almost everyone falls into it when they self-study.

Let me break down what's specifically happening, and then give you a concrete system that fixes it.

---

## Diagnosis: five things going wrong

**1. You're consuming way more than you're producing.**
Watching YouTube, doing Duolingo lessons, watching piano tutorials — these feel like learning, but they're passive. Learning actually happens when you pull the information *out* of your head (speaking a sentence cold, playing a measure without looking at the tutorial, etc.), not when you put it in. A good rule of thumb: you should be producing (speaking, writing, playing, solving) **at least 3x as much time as you spend consuming.** Most beginners are inverted — 5x more input than output. That's you.

**2. Duolingo and "watching videos" both fake the feedback loop.**
Duolingo tells you you're doing great because you tapped the right tile from a set of four. That's recognition, not recall. Recognition is about 10x easier than recall, and only recall generalizes to "I can produce this from scratch in Mexico." Similarly, watching a YouTuber play Chopin teaches you nothing about *your* hands doing it.

**3. You're skipping prerequisites and jumping to the cool thing.**
A Chopin piece is not a starting skill — it's an *encompassing* skill that assumes hand independence, fluency reading both clefs, basic scale patterns, dynamics, pedaling, and probably 50 more things. Attempting it without those is like trying to write a novel when you don't know the alphabet. You don't feel it's premature because tutorials can walk you through each note, but you never built the underlying automaticity, so the moment the scaffolding is gone, so is the skill. Same thing with Spanish: jumping to "understand native speakers in Mexico" without ~1500 words of vocab solidly owned and basic grammar automated is a guaranteed crash.

**4. No spaced review, so everything you "learned" decayed.**
Human memory is leaky by default. If you learn a word or a chord and don't retrieve it again at expanding intervals (1 day, 3 days, 1 week, 2 weeks, a month), it's gone. Duolingo pretends to do this but is too shallow and too recognition-based to actually work. Without explicit spaced retrieval, at week 8 you literally cannot access what you "learned" in week 2 — it's not a motivation problem, it's a memory-decay problem, and it absolutely crushes morale.

**5. The goals were vague, so there was no real "am I on track?" signal.**
"Learn Spanish" and "learn piano" are not goals. They have no bar. Without a concrete near-term target ("hold a 5-minute conversation about food by week 8") there's no way to tell whether you're progressing or drifting — so after a few weeks of not feeling obvious progress, your brain (correctly) concludes this isn't working and bails.

These five things compound. The enthusiasm phase masks all of them. The plateau around week 4 is when they become visible and you quit. Nothing is wrong with your discipline. The *method* is broken.

---

## The fix: a system, not more willpower

The principle is simple: **front-load production, build in forced retrieval, keep the bar concrete, and let consumption be a short scaffold rather than the main activity.**

Here's a template that applies to any skill-based domain. Then I'll instantiate it for Spanish and piano specifically.

### The five rules

1. **One concrete goal with a deadline.** Not "learn Spanish." "By June 1, I can hold a 15-minute conversation with an iTalki tutor about food, family, and work, using past and present tense, with occasional hesitation but no breakdowns." You have to be able to tell if you hit it.

2. **Pick ONE primary resource and actually use it.** Not five apps + three YouTube channels + two textbooks. Hoarding tabs is a form of procrastination. One resource, one curriculum, one direction.

3. **Every session must have active production, in a ratio of ~3:1 over consumption.** If your 30 minutes is 25 minutes of watching and 5 minutes of trying, flip it. Watch 5, try 25.

4. **Every session must have spaced retrieval of old material.** Even 5 minutes at the start of the session — "what did I learn 3 days ago, 1 week ago, 2 weeks ago, without looking." This is the single biggest technique nobody uses.

5. **Get fast feedback on every attempt.** You need to know within seconds-to-minutes whether you did it right. That means an answer key, a tutor, a recording you play back, an SRS app that actually checks recall, etc. Duolingo tapping doesn't count.

### The daily session shape (30 min is enough)

- **Minutes 0–7: Spaced retrieval warm-up.** Pull old stuff from memory, no notes. Check. The things you miss get queued for tomorrow.
- **Minutes 7–22: Active production on new or recent material.** Speak sentences out loud, play the hard 4 bars, write the code, whatever "doing it" means in the domain. Consume only enough to get unstuck (a couple of minutes of explanation or a worked example), then get back to producing.
- **Minutes 22–27: Interleaved mixed practice.** Mix in stuff from the last few weeks, not just today's topic. This is the part that feels hardest and least productive. It is the part that actually makes knowledge stick.
- **Minutes 27–30: Brief self-check.** What did you miss today? Write it down in one line. Tomorrow's warm-up starts there.

Do this 5–6 days a week. Consistency at 30 min beats heroic 2-hour sessions on weekends — the spacing is what compounds.

### The weekly shape

- One longer session (45–60 min) where you do something that resembles the real goal: have a conversation with a tutor, play a piece end-to-end and record it, ship a small piece of real work. This is your "does this work in the wild" test. It will expose gaps and those gaps become next week's targeted practice.
- Record and timestamp something monthly so you can *see* progress. Progress in these domains is non-linear and largely invisible day to day; monthly recordings are how you convince yourself it's working when the day-to-day feels flat.

## Applied: what a fixed Spanish plan looks like

Since you already tried Spanish, let me show what it should have looked like. If you go back to Spanish, do this:

**Goal:** In 6 months, hold a 30-minute conversation with an iTalki tutor on everyday topics (food, family, travel, work, hobbies) with past and present tense, with some hesitation but no breakdowns.

**Daily (25–30 min):**
- 0–8 min: Anki reviews. A frequency deck of the top 5000 Spanish words, capped at **10 new cards per day** (the #1 failure mode of Anki is adding too many). Never skip.
- 8–20 min: Output production. Pick today's grammar point or vocab set. Write or say aloud 5 sentences using it about your actual life today. Record yourself on your phone. Listen back.
- 20–28 min: Comprehensible input with forced output. Listen to a 2-minute clip from *Dreaming Spanish* (start at Superbeginner level). Stop and summarize out loud what you just heard, without replaying. This is forced retrieval of input, which is where listening comprehension actually forms.

**Weekly (25 min):**
- One iTalki tutor session. This is the single highest-leverage 25 minutes of the week — it's your live test, your feedback source, and the generator of new Anki cards from words you didn't know. Yes it's uncomfortable. That's why it works.

**Drop immediately:**
- Duolingo. It's gamified recognition, not production. It gave you the *feeling* of progress that exploded when you hit reality in Mexico.
- Binge-watching "Learn Spanish" YouTube content. A few minutes of Language Transfer is fine as a grammar primer. More than 10 min/day of video is you hiding.

**What was specifically wrong with your Mexico trip:** you had input-only practice, no production, no retrieval, no native-speaker interaction before you arrived. The trip wasn't the test that revealed you hadn't learned — the trip was the *first time* you'd actually tried to produce. Of course it collapsed.

## Applied: what a fixed piano plan looks like

**Goal:** In 12 months, perform a Bach 2-part invention (start with #1 in C) OR a simple Chopin prelude (#7 in A — actually playable at this level; most of the famous Chopin is not) at tempo, musically, from memory.

**Daily (30 min):**
- 0–5 min: Warm-up. A scale (rotating through keys) and a simple Hanon exercise. Builds finger automaticity — this is the "low-level skills must become unconscious" layer that makes everything else possible.
- 5–20 min: Deliberate practice on ONE specific hard passage — usually 4 bars, sometimes 2. Play it absurdly slowly (half tempo or slower) until perfect. Then build tempo. **Never play the piece from the top over and over** — that's blocked passive practice and it's mostly wasted time.
- 20–27 min: Interleaving. Revisit a previous piece from memory (retrieval), or sight-read something new and easy, or work on a scale in a new key.
- 27–30 min: Write down the one specific mistake you drilled today. Tomorrow's warm-up starts with it.

**Weekly:** Record yourself playing current pieces end-to-end. Listen back. The worst 8 bars become next week's deliberate practice focus.

**Monthly (if budget allows):** A single 30-minute lesson with a real teacher. Even one per month catches technique drift that you literally cannot see or hear in yourself. Not essential, but very high leverage.

**On the Chopin thing specifically:** most Chopin is genuinely intermediate-to-advanced. Attempting it as a beginner is what killed your motivation — the gap between "I can see what to do" and "my hands can do it" was uncrossable without the prerequisites. Start with Alfred's Adult All-in-One or Faber Adult Piano Adventures for 6 months. When the book is done, you'll have the motor foundation to actually attack a real piece. The "boring beginner book" is the fastest path to the Chopin, not a detour from it.

## What to expect (this part is important)

The new method is going to feel **worse than what you were doing before**, especially in the first 2–3 weeks. Here's why, and why that's not a reason to quit:

- Retrieving from memory feels harder than watching a video. It is harder. That's the point — the effort is the mechanism by which memory forms. If it felt easy, it wouldn't be working.
- You'll make more visible mistakes than you did with Duolingo, because the new method exposes what you actually know instead of hiding it. More mistakes = more learning, counterintuitively.
- Interleaving (mixing topics) will feel less productive than blocked practice (drilling one thing). Studies repeatedly show people *predict their own test scores too high* after blocked practice and accurately after interleaved. Trust the method over the feeling.
- There will be a plateau around week 3–4 that looks identical to the one that made you quit before. The difference is that with spaced retrieval and active production, the plateau ends and the next phase genuinely unlocks. With pure consumption, the plateau was real — you weren't learning. Now the plateau is just the uncomfortable middle.

A useful signal: **if your method feels fluent and fun and fast, you're probably not learning.** If it feels effortful and error-prone and slow, you probably are. This is the single most important reframe in all of cognitive learning science. It's called *desirable difficulty.*

---

## Pick one

One last thing. Don't restart both Spanish and piano at once. Pick ONE and do it for 3 months with this system before adding anything. The reason previous attempts failed wasn't that you didn't have enough going on — it was that the method didn't stick. Prove the method works on one domain first. Once the system is habitual (spaced retrieval, active production, one concrete goal), you can spin up a second domain at a lower time budget. But not before.

Which one matters more to you right now? Pick that, set the goal concretely, and start tomorrow morning with 30 minutes. Don't spend a week "researching the best resources" — that's the consumption trap wearing a disguise. Pick the primary resource from this document (Anki + Dreaming Spanish + iTalki tutor, or Alfred's + a monthly teacher), start, and fix details as you go.
