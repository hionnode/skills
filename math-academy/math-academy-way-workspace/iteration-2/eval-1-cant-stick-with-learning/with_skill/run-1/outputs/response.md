# What's actually going wrong, and how to fix it

Nothing is wrong with you. You're running a method that *feels* like learning but doesn't produce it, and the gap between the two doesn't reveal itself until you try to actually use the skill. Once you see the pattern, the fix is pretty mechanical. Let me diagnose, then prescribe.

## The diagnosis: you're stuck in the consumption loop

Here's the pattern in both the Spanish and the piano stories:

1. **Consumption without production.** Duolingo taps, YouTube videos, watching someone play Chopin — all of that is *input*. Almost none of it is *output* (speaking Spanish to a human, playing a piece cold without the tutorial paused on your screen). Learning happens when you produce from memory, not when you follow along. Following along produces what cognitive scientists call the *illusion of comprehension* — it feels like you're learning, because at each moment you understand what's happening. But understanding in the moment isn't the same as being able to do it unassisted later. Mexico was the first time anything forced you into production mode, and production mode is where the real state of your skill showed up.

2. **No defined target.** "Learn Spanish" and "learn piano" aren't targets, they're directions. You can't tell if you're close, so you can't tell if the plan is working, so you can't correct course. A real target is: "hold a 15-minute conversation with a taxi driver in Mexico about where I'm going and why" or "play Chopin's Op. 28 No. 4 at tempo, from memory, cleanly." You'd know instantly whether you'd arrived.

3. **Jumping to the shiny thing instead of building the foundation.** You picked a Chopin piece because it sounded cool. That's a completely reasonable motivation but it's a stage-3 task attempted at stage-1 fluency. It's the equivalent of wanting to learn Spanish by reading García Márquez. The piece requires sub-skills (independent hand coordination, sight-reading basic rhythms, pedaling, dynamic control) you hadn't built yet, and when those sub-skills are missing, the top-level task is pure frustration. Same with Spanish: Duolingo's phrase drills don't cover conversational recovery, listening under speed, or the ~1500 highest-frequency words, which is what you actually needed in Mexico.

4. **No feedback loop.** With Duolingo you got right/wrong on artificial sentences. With YouTube piano you got nothing. Nobody caught when your hand position was slightly off in week 2, which is why it was catastrophically off by week 6 on the Chopin. Learning without feedback is basically performance theater — you put in the hours but drift in directions you can't see.

5. **No external accountability.** You're a party of one. On Tuesday in week 4, when you're tired and it's easier to watch a show, nobody notices if you skip. Motivation is a trait that fluctuates; accountability is a structure that doesn't. The research on high-performing learners is unsentimental about this: almost nobody sustains effortful practice at intensity purely on their own will. They have coaches, tutors, teachers, parents, deadlines, or public commitments structuring the work. "Lone self-driven learner" is mostly a myth.

6. **(Probably) wrong stage for the activity.** If you picked up piano "because it sounded cool" and not because you have to, you're a stage-1 hobbyist — someone who should be *exploring and enjoying* the domain, not drilling like a conservatory student. Month 1 of adult piano shouldn't be a Chopin piece. It should be a dozen songs you half-learn for fun, to find out what you actually love about the instrument, and *then* commit to practicing seriously. Forcing deliberate-practice mode in stage 1 is how keyboards end up in corners.

The good news: none of these are character failings. They're design failures in how the learning was structured. Fix the structure and the "I always quit" pattern stops.

## The prescription: a system you can drop any new skill into

Here's the structure. I'll lay it out generically, then show you how I'd apply it to a re-attempt at Spanish (since you have a concrete target case) and how it differs for piano.

### 1. Pick a concrete target you can test against

Not "learn Spanish." Something like: **"by month 6, hold a 30-minute conversation in Spanish with a native speaker about everyday topics — food, family, work, travel, hobbies — with occasional hesitation but no communication breakdowns."** (This is roughly B1 on the CEFR, and it's what you thought you were building toward in Mexico.)

For piano, if you come back to it: **"in 6 months, play three pieces I actually enjoy, cleanly, from memory, at tempo, for a friend."** Not Chopin Op. 28. Something at your real level — a simplified pop arrangement, a Bach minuet, an easy Satie. Pick pieces that are one small step above your current reach, not five steps above.

You'll notice both of these have a **who sees it** clause baked in: a native speaker, a friend. That's deliberate — it's the test that forces real production.

### 2. Book the forcing function *before* you start

This is the single highest-leverage move and the one you skipped both times.

**For Spanish:** book a trip, or commit to a specific conversation event (e.g., a Spanish meetup in your city on a specific Saturday 3 months out, or a Mexico trip 6 months out with a pre-booked iTalki tutor session during it). Tell someone. Book non-refundable flights if the trip is the forcing function. The key property: the date cannot be moved without embarrassment or lost money.

**For piano:** commit to playing for a specific person on a specific date. Your partner's birthday. A friend's housewarming in four months. A 60-second video you'll post on a specific Sunday. Again: someone else is watching and the date can't slide.

Without a forcing function, a 6-month plan is just a daydream. With one, Tuesday-in-week-4 you has a reason to sit down.

### 3. Get a human in the loop on a recurring cadence

If you take away one thing, take this. An hour with a tutor every one or two weeks is worth more than any other single thing you can add. They catch mistakes before they calcify, they push you at exactly your edge, and — crucially — the fact that they're going to see your work on Thursday is why you do the work on Wednesday.

**For Spanish:** an iTalki tutor, $15-25/hour, one 30-minute session per week. Not a class. A one-on-one conversation session. Tell them your goal; they'll drive you toward it.

**For piano:** a piano teacher, even if only every two weeks. Alternatively, a structured app with real feedback (not just "press this key now" games) plus a video check-in with a teacher monthly.

If cost is a real constraint, the substitutes, in descending order of quality, are: a study partner working on the same material with a fixed check-in cadence; a Discord or community with a weekly report culture; a daily streak tracker (Anki, a habit app) paired with a public commitment. Don't settle for "I'll track myself in a spreadsheet." That's the setup you had last time.

### 4. Design the daily session so it's ~70% production, ~15% intake

This is the big reversal. Flip the ratio you were running.

A good session structure for a skill like this (adapt the minutes to your time budget):

- **Warm-up (5-10 min):** review yesterday's material *from memory*, no notes. Write/speak/play it cold, then check.
- **New material (15-25 min):** a *tiny* amount of new input — one grammar point, one chord progression, one short video segment — followed immediately by production on that new thing. Rule of thumb: for every 1 minute consuming, spend 3 minutes producing. If you find yourself 15 minutes into a video having not produced anything, stop and produce.
- **Interleaved mixed practice (10-15 min):** problems/phrases/exercises drawn from a *mix* of stuff you've already learned, not just today's topic. This feels harder than drilling today's thing 20 times, and that's exactly why it works — it trains you to pull the right tool from a full toolbox, which is what real use demands.
- **Retrieval quiz (2-5 min, optional but potent):** a couple of from-memory questions. "How do I say 'I used to live in Chicago'?" Answer out loud, then check.

For Spanish specifically, I'd replace your prior stack like this:

- **Drop Duolingo** as the primary. It's gamified in ways that don't produce fluency — it trains tap-matching, not speaking. Keep it at most as a consistency floor for streak-sake.
- **Primary tool: Anki** with a Spanish frequency deck (top 5000 words), plus sentence cards you build yourself from sentences the tutor corrects. 10 new cards/day maximum. Never skip reviews.
- **Input with output: Dreaming Spanish** podcast segments — pause every 1-2 minutes and summarize aloud what was just said. This is listening plus forced production.
- **Grammar scaffolding: Language Transfer** (free, audio, excellent pedagogy). Short sessions.
- **Weekly iTalki conversation** with a tutor — the highest-value 30 minutes of your week. Every word you stumble on becomes a new Anki card.

For piano, I'd structure month 1 as pure exploration — try a dozen easy-but-fun songs, don't finish most of them, find out what you love. Only *after* that, if you still want to commit, introduce structured practice: 5 minutes scales, 10 minutes sight-reading at a level you can barely handle, 10 minutes working a small section of an actually-achievable piece slowly with a metronome, 5 minutes reviewing yesterday's piece from memory. Chopin comes in year 2 or 3, not month 1.

### 5. Define mastery operationally, and don't move on before hitting it

"I kind of get it" is the trap. A working definition: you can do five consecutive representative examples, from memory, without hints, at normal pace. If any of those break — you peek, you slip, you're way too slow — it's not mastered. Re-queue and come back.

This is why you felt like you were learning Spanish and then couldn't produce anything in Mexico: nothing had actually been pushed past the "I kind of get it" threshold. There's no Mexico trip where you get graded on multiple choice.

### 6. Catch the drift with spaced review + a weekly mixed quiz

Every day, do a short spaced review of older material (Anki handles this automatically for vocab; for other skills you can do it manually with a list). Every week, a 15-minute mixed practice / quiz on everything from the past 2-4 weeks. Anything you miss goes back into the review queue with a shortened interval. This is what stops the silent decay that ate your Spanish between weeks 4 and 12.

### 7. Define a minimum viable session for bad days

The main reason 3-month plans die isn't one bad day — it's that one bad day turns into a week off, which turns into "I've already broken the streak, why bother." Prevent this by defining the smallest thing that still counts as a session: 10 minutes of Anki reviews, or 5 minutes of scales. That's the commitment on the worst day. The streak survives; the habit survives; you resume normal intensity tomorrow.

### 8. Name your specific anti-patterns up front

For you specifically, based on what you described, the anti-patterns to watch:

- **"Just one more video."** If you've consumed for 15 minutes without producing, the next thing you do is produce. Close the tab.
- **Picking the shiny hard piece over the foundation.** If something sounds cool but you can't do the sub-skills, park it. Come back when it's one step above your level, not five.
- **Confusing comprehension with competence.** "I get it when I see it" is not the test. "I can do it cold, from nothing, tomorrow" is the test.
- **Skipping the tutor/check-in when you feel unprepared.** That's exactly when the check-in is most valuable — the unpreparedness is the data.
- **Quitting because "it got hard in week 3."** Week 3 is supposed to feel harder than week 1. You're building retrieval fatigue on the old material while adding new material. The plan doesn't become wrong just because it starts feeling hard — that's the sign it's actually loading your memory rather than sliding past it. The real "something's wrong" signals are different: two milestone failures in a row, or a growing review backlog that won't shrink, or three full weeks without detectable progress on the goal.

### 9. A note on how this will feel

This setup will feel *worse* than your prior approach for the first 2-3 weeks. You'll have more moments of "I don't know this," more visible mistakes, more mild frustration. That's not a sign it's failing; that's the sign it's working. The old approach felt great and produced nothing; this one will feel harder and produce a real skill. The payoff shows up on the delayed test — the conversation with a stranger, the performance for a friend — not in the moment.

## If I were you, this week

Concretely, to break the cycle now:

1. **Pick one skill.** Not two. Spanish, I'd suggest, since you have unfinished business and a concrete goal.
2. **Book a forcing function this week.** A trip, a meetup, a specific Spanish-only dinner with a bilingual friend, a scheduled call in Spanish with a family member or friend — something on a calendar within 4-6 months.
3. **Book a weekly iTalki tutor.** Trial it for 4 weeks before judging. If the first tutor isn't good, try a different one — don't give up on the format.
4. **Set up Anki** with a frequency deck, cap at 10 new cards/day.
5. **Define your minimum viable session** (10 min of Anki + say 3 sentences out loud about your day = a session).
6. **Pick your habit-stack time and place.** "I do my session at [specific time] at [specific place]." Ambiguity about when is the number one killer.
7. **Tell one specific person** what you're doing and your forcing-function date.

That's the whole thing. Notice there's zero new content to consume before you start — the setup is mostly structural. You don't need more information, you need a different shape of practice and a structure that holds you in it.

One last thing: the fact that you *can* notice this pattern and want to break it is actually the hard part. The fix is mechanical.
